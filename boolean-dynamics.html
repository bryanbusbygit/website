Boolean Monomial Dynamics

Elspas's 1959 analysis of linear sequential networks relates periodic behavior to matrix algebra over finite fields [1]. Nonlinear updates generally lack this linear description. Boolean monomial systems retain enough structure to study their transients through directed walks, connecting the problem to primitive matrices and the arithmetic of cycle lengths.

Definition 1 (Boolean dynamical system)

A Boolean dynamical system on \(n\) variables is a map \(f:\{0,1\}^n\to\{0,1\}^n\). A state \(a=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)\) records one binary value per variable, and \(f=(f_1,\ldots,f_n)\) updates all variables simultaneously at each integer time \(t\geq0\) according to

\[ a_i(t+1)=f_i(a_1(t),\ldots,a_n(t)). \]

Iterating the rule gives the orbit \(a,f(a),f^2(a),\ldots\), where \(f^m\) means \(m\) successive applications of \(f\), and \(f^0\) is the identity. The state graph has all \(2^n\) states as vertices and one arrow \(a\to f(a)\) from each state. A fixed point satisfies \(f(a)=a\); a periodic orbit returns to its starting state after some positive number of updates. Its period is the smallest such number.

Every orbit eventually enters a periodic orbit because the state set is finite. Define \(\tau(a)\) to be the number of updates before it first reaches that orbit. The system’s maximum transient length is \(t(f)=\max_{a\in\{0,1\}^n}\tau(a)\). In particular, a state already on a periodic orbit has \(\tau(a)=0\).

A state graph consists of directed cycles with trees feeding into them. The examples below illustrate this structure. Our aim is to recover the maximum transient from the update rule without enumerating every state.

Definition 2 (Boolean monomial system)

A Boolean system is monomial when each update is a product of selected input values, possibly multiplied by \(0\). We study the case with no identically zero coordinates. For a subset \(S_i\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\), its coordinate update is \(f_i(a)=\prod_{j\in S_i}a_j\).

The product is the logical AND of its inputs. An empty product is the constant \(1\). Since \(a_j^2=a_j\) for \(a_j\in\{0,1\}\), each variable need appear at most once. Such a monomial is squarefree.

Algebraically, \(\{0,1\}\) forms the field \(\mathbb F_2\) with addition and multiplication modulo \(2\). For example, \(f=(a_2,a_1a_3,a_4,a_2)\) sends \((1,0,1,1)\) to \((0,1,1,0)\) in one simultaneous update.

The dependency graph \(D_f\) has one vertex per variable and an arrow \(j\to i\) when \(j\in S_i\), meaning that \(f_i\) uses \(a_j\). A directed walk follows arrows in order and may repeat vertices or edges; its length is the number of edges traversed. A closed walk returns to its starting vertex, and a directed cycle has no other repeated vertex. Unlike the state graph, \(D_f\) has only \(n\) vertices.

Theorem 1 (Dependencies and directed walks)

For a Boolean monomial system with nonzero squarefree coordinates and \(m\geq1\), the variable \(a_j\) occurs in \((f^m)_i\) if and only if a directed walk of length \(m\) joins \(j\) to \(i\) in \(D_f\) [3].

Let \(A\) be the adjacency matrix of \(D_f\), with \(A_{ij}=1\) when \(j\to i\) and \(0\) otherwise. Then \((A^m)_{ij}>0\) exactly when a length-\(m\) walk joins \(j\) to \(i\). Matrix powers here use ordinary integer arithmetic, with rows indexing destinations and columns sources. Matrix powers encode dependencies rather than linearize the update.

Lemma 1 (Propagation of zeros)

Under the hypotheses of Theorem 1, \((f^m(a))_i=0\) if and only if some vertex \(j\) with \(a_j=0\) has a directed walk of length \(m\) to \(i\).

The transient is therefore governed by the lengths at which dependencies occur. A graph is strongly connected if a directed walk joins every ordered pair of vertices. Even then, some lengths may be unavailable between a given pair. The graph is primitive when it is strongly connected and the greatest common divisor (gcd) of its cycle lengths is \(1\). Its exponent \(\gamma(D_f)\) is the least power for which every matrix entry is positive [7].

Theorem 2 (Transient length and exponent)

Let \(f\) be a Boolean monomial system with nonzero squarefree coordinates in \(n\geq2\) variables. If \(D_f\) is primitive, its exponent equals the maximum transient length [5].

\[ t(f)=\gamma(D_f). \]

At this time every coordinate depends on every initial variable, sending each state containing a zero to the all-zero fixed point.

Estimating this exponent requires understanding how traversing cycles supplies additional steps. Let \(c_1,\ldots,c_k\) be the distinct directed-cycle lengths. Their nonnegative integer combinations form

\[ \begin{aligned} \Gamma&=\langle c_1,\ldots,c_k\rangle\\ &=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^k m_i c_i\;\middle|\;m_i\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}\right\}. \end{aligned} \]

Here \(\mathbb Z_{\geq0}\) denotes the nonnegative integers. A numerical semigroup is a set of nonnegative integers containing \(0\), closed under addition, and missing only finitely many nonnegative integers. For a primitive graph, the cycle lengths have gcd \(1\), so \(\Gamma\) is such a semigroup [4]. A gap is a nonnegative integer outside it. Its largest gap is the Frobenius number \(\mathrm{ℊ}\), with \(\mathrm{ℊ}=-1\) when there are no gaps.

Theorem 3 (Sylvester's formula)

If \(a,b>1\) are coprime integers (their gcd is \(1\)), the largest integer outside \(\langle a,b\rangle\) is [8]

\[ \mathrm{ℊ}=ab-a-b. \]

The directed distance from \(u\) to \(v\) is the length of a shortest directed path from \(u\) to \(v\). The diameter \(d=\operatorname{diam}(D_f)\) is the greatest of these distances over all ordered pairs of vertices. The approach in the 2024 poster [6] combines this travel distance with the gaps measured by the Frobenius number.

Proposed bounds (Diameter and Frobenius number)

Let \(f\) be a Boolean monomial system with nonzero squarefree coordinates and primitive dependency graph \(D_f\). Write \(d=\operatorname{diam}(D_f)\), and let \(\mathrm{ℊ}\) be the Frobenius number of the semigroup generated by its cycle lengths. The poster aims to establish, under suitable hypotheses on the graph, the lower and upper bounds

\[ \begin{aligned} 1+\mathrm{ℊ}+d&\leq t(f),\\ t(f)&\leq \mathrm{ℊ}+2d. \end{aligned} \]

The guiding idea is to express walk lengths as a path length plus a nonnegative combination of cycle lengths. The Frobenius number describes the missing combinations, while the diameter measures travel between vertices. Making this description work for prescribed starting and ending vertices is the central step toward the proposed bounds.

Families with exact transient formulas

Our manuscript in preparation gives exact formulas for two families of dependency graphs. Throughout, the Boolean monomial systems have no identically zero coordinate functions, so Theorem 2 translates each graph exponent into \(t(f)\).

A cycle bouquet is formed by taking directed cycles and identifying one vertex from each into a single shared vertex, with no other intersections. Let their lengths be \(2\leq c_1<\cdots<c_k\), with gcd \(1\), and let \(L=c_k\) be the longest length. Write \(\mathrm{ℊ}\) for the Frobenius number of \(\langle c_1,\ldots,c_k\rangle\).

Theorem 4 (Cycle bouquets)

If \(D_f\) is a cycle bouquet with the lengths and hypotheses above, then

\[ t(f)=\mathrm{ℊ}+2L-1. \]

This covers any number of distinct cycle lengths with gcd \(1\). For two coprime lengths \(p<q\), Sylvester’s formula makes it completely explicit, giving \(t(f)=pq-p+q-1\). With more generators, the bouquet formula is exact once the corresponding Frobenius number is known.

The second family joins three directed cycles of lengths \(2\leq p<q<r\) at two shared vertices. The \(p\)- and \(q\)-cycles meet only at \(u\), and the \(q\)- and \(r\)-cycles meet only at \(v\), with no other intersections. On the \(q\)-cycle, the directed segment from \(u\) to \(v\) has length \(q-1\), followed by the edge \(v\to u\). Denote this graph by \(G(p,q,r)\).

Theorem 5 (Three cycles joined at two vertices)

Suppose \(D_f=G(p,q,r)\), \(\gcd(p,q)=1\), and \(r-q\in\langle p,q\rangle\). Thus \(r-q\) is a nonnegative integer combination of \(p\) and \(q\). With \(\mathrm{ℊ}=pq-p-q\),

\[ \begin{aligned} t(f)&=\mathrm{ℊ}+q+2r-1\\ &=pq-p+2r-1. \end{aligned} \]

For example, lengths \(2,3,5\) satisfy these hypotheses. Joining all three cycles at one vertex gives \(t(f)=10\), while the specified arrangement at two vertices gives \(t(f)=13\). The cycle lengths agree, but their placement changes the transient. The second formula applies to the arrangement and arithmetic conditions stated above.

What we don’t know

The exact formulas above cover bouquets and the specified family of three cycles joined at two vertices. How far can these formulas be extended to other arrangements of cycles? Which hypotheses make the poster’s proposed bounds valid? Primitivity alone is insufficient. Cycles of the needed lengths may occur at different locations, and reaching them can require extra steps. We need to identify graph families where the proposed decomposition of walk lengths accounts for this travel.

For those families, when is either bound attained? Understanding the equality cases would show when the diameter and cycle-length arithmetic determine the transient exactly, and when the way cycles are joined contributes further information.
