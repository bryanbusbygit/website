Boolean Monomial Dynamics

Every orbit of a map on a finite set is eventually periodic. We study the maximum transient length of Boolean monomial dynamical systems by relating their iterates to directed walks in the dependency digraph. For the classes of digraphs considered here, acyclic subdigraphs, connecting-path distances, and arithmetic properties of cycle lengths yield lower and upper bounds on the time required to reach a periodic orbit.

Transients

Boolean networks provide discrete models of gene regulation in which binary variables represent gene activity and coordinate update functions encode regulatory interactions. [3] Periodic orbits represent persistent activity patterns. The transient length of an initial state is the least number of iterations required to reach a periodic state; it is zero precisely when the initial state is periodic.

The state-transition graph has an edge from each state to its image and determines all transient lengths. A system on \(n\) Boolean variables, however, has \(2^n\) states, so explicit construction of this graph grows exponentially with \(n\). We instead seek bounds derived directly from the coordinate functions and their dependency structure.

The dependency digraph

Let \(f:\{0,1\}^n\to\{0,1\}^n\) be a Boolean monomial map, with state vector \(\mathbf x=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\) and coordinate functions \(f_1,\ldots,f_n\). For each \(i\), an index set \(N_i\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\) specifies the variables in the coordinate monomial:

\[ f_i(\mathbf x)=\prod_{j\in N_i}x_j. \]

Each product is the logical conjunction of its factors. The empty product is taken to be \(1\), and all coordinates are updated simultaneously.

The dependency digraph \(D_f\) has vertex set \(\{1,\ldots,n\}\) and an edge \(i\to j\) if and only if \(j\in N_i\), equivalently, if \(x_j\) divides \(f_i\). We use the output-to-input convention: an edge points from a coordinate to a variable on which its update depends.

Since the sets \(N_i\) determine the coordinate functions, \(D_f\) encodes the system on \(n\) vertices. Colón-Reyes, Laubenbacher, and Pareigis used this representation to study the periodic behavior of Boolean monomial systems. [2]

For example, consider

\[ f(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)=(x_2,\,x_1x_3,\,x_4,\,x_2). \]

Here \(N_2=\{1,3\}\), giving the edges \(2\to1\) and \(2\to3\); the remaining coordinate functions give \(1\to2\), \(3\to4\), and \(4\to2\).

Iterates and directed walks

Successive substitution expresses each coordinate of an iterate in terms of the initial variables. In the displayed example, \((f^2)_2(\mathbf x)=f_1(\mathbf x)f_3(\mathbf x)=x_2x_4\). The corresponding length-two walks from vertex \(2\) are \(2\to1\to2\) and \(2\to3\to4\), whose endpoints index precisely the variables occurring in \((f^2)_2\).

More generally, let \(f^m\) denote \(m\) successive applications of \(f\), and let \((f^m)_i\) be its \(i\)th coordinate. For \(m\geq1\), the variable \(x_j\) occurs in \((f^m)_i\) if and only if \(D_f\) has a directed walk of length \(m\) from \(i\) to \(j\). A directed walk may repeat vertices and edges, and its length is the number of edges traversed. Multiple walks with the same endpoint may produce repeated factors under substitution, but these reduce to a single factor by the Boolean identity \(x_j^2=x_j\). A related correspondence holds for monomial systems over arbitrary finite fields. [4]

This correspondence reduces the analysis of iterates to the attainable walk lengths between ordered pairs of vertices. Let \(\tau_f(\mathbf x)\) denote the transient length of an initial state \(\mathbf x\). The maximum transient length is

\[ \tau_{\max}(f)=\max_{\mathbf x\in\{0,1\}^n}\tau_f(\mathbf x). \]

Paths and cycles

Directed cycles determine how walks can be extended without changing their endpoints. Inserting \(q\) traversals of an accessible cycle of length \(c\) increases a walk's length by \(qc\). When several cycles are accessible along a walk, repeated traversals contribute nonnegative integer combinations of their lengths.

We organize the analysis by deleting selected edges to obtain an acyclic subdigraph. Every directed walk in this subdigraph is a path and consequently has bounded length. Restoring the deleted edges permits repeated cycle traversals; the resulting walk lengths depend on both cycle multiplicities and the lengths of paths connecting the cycles.

For cycle lengths \(c_1,\ldots,c_s\) with greatest common divisor \(1\), the nonnegative integer combinations \(q_1c_1+\cdots+q_sc_s\) form a numerical semigroup. Its Frobenius number is the largest integer not expressible as a nonnegative integer linear combination of the cycle lengths. Every larger integer admits such a representation. [5]

For example, \(2\) and \(3\) generate every integer at least \(2\), but do not generate \(1\), so their Frobenius number is \(1\). Applying this arithmetic criterion to walk lengths also requires accessibility of the relevant cycles and inclusion of the lengths of the connecting paths.

Bounds on the transient

For the dependency digraphs considered in our work, graph distances account for connecting paths, while Frobenius numbers control gaps in attainable walk lengths. The correspondence between walks and coordinate monomials translates these estimates into lower and upper bounds on \(\tau_{\max}(f)\).

In one example treated in the current work, the bounds are

\[ 9\leq \tau_{\max}(f)\leq15. \]

Hence at least one initial state has transient length at least nine, while every initial state reaches a periodic orbit within fifteen iterations. These estimates apply under the hypotheses of the corresponding graph analysis; they do not constitute a closed formula for arbitrary Boolean monomial systems.
