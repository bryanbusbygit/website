Arithmetical Structures on Graphs

Arithmetical structures generalize the graph Laplacian by replacing vertex degrees with positive integers while retaining a positive integer kernel vector. Originating in Lorenzini's study of degenerating curves, they connect integer linear algebra with graph enumeration [3]. This project studies cycles with one multi-edge through subdivision and a modified Euclidean algorithm.

This Laplacian generalization is expressed by a kernel equation. For adjacency matrix \(A\), an arithmetical structure is a pair of positive integer vectors \((\mathbf d,\mathbf r)\) satisfying

\[ (\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d)-A)\mathbf r=0, \qquad \gcd(\mathbf r)=1. \]

Each \(r\)-label divides the sum of its neighbors, counting edge multiplicity, and the quotient is its \(d\)-label. The gcd condition removes scaled copies. The assignment \(\mathbf r=\mathbf1\), \(\mathbf d=\deg\) recovers the ordinary Laplacian. Although the integer labels have no prescribed bound, every fixed connected graph admits finitely many structures [3], but their enumeration depends on its geometry. For an \(n\)-cycle with fixed vertices, the count is \(\binom{2n-1}{n-1}\) [1].

A multi-edge changes the divisibility conditions at its two endpoints. To isolate this arithmetic, the remaining path can be studied through subdivision and its inverse, smoothing. Subdivision inserts the label \(a+b\) along an ordinary edge labeled \(a,b\), assigns the new quotient \(1\), and increases both endpoint quotients by \(1\). These operations preserve normalization and the critical group [1]. On a multi-edge cycle, smoothing applies away from the multi-edge when at least four vertices remain before removal. Structures whose ordinary vertices all have quotients greater than \(1\) are smooth.

The endpoint data constrain which reduced structures can occur. If \(a,b\) label the multi-edge endpoints and \(k\) is its multiplicity, then \(a,b,k\) must be pairwise coprime [Proposition 2.1]. Conversely, pairwise coprime \(a,b>1\), \(k\geq2\) determine a construction. Starting from \((kb,a)\) and \((ka,b)\), append to consecutive terms \(p,q\) the least nonnegative remainder \(s\equiv-p\pmod q\), stopping before zero. The equation \(p+s=dq\) enforces local divisibility. Discarding the initial terms, joining \(a,b\) by \(k\) edges, and identifying the terminal \(1\)'s gives a cycle with the prescribed endpoints.

This construction supplies labelings, but its remainder sequences determine the cycle's size. Enumeration at a fixed size therefore requires classifying endpoint data and subdivisions that produce a prescribed size without duplication. The manuscript with Nesty Dogbatse and Peter Palma develops this approach, extending methods used for paths with a doubled edge [2].
