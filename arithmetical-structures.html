Arithmetical Structures on Graphs

Arithmetical structures generalize the graph Laplacian by allowing its diagonal entries to vary while retaining a positive integral kernel vector. We study their enumeration on cycles with a multi-edge, using subdivision and a modified Euclidean algorithm.

The count is known for ordinary cycles. Replacing one edge by several parallel edges changes the divisibility equations at just two vertices. The remaining equations are those of a path, so the problem is to determine which path labelings satisfy the new endpoint conditions.

Let \(G\) be a finite connected undirected loopless graph with at least two vertices and adjacency matrix \(A\), counting multiplicities. Each vertex label must divide the sum of its neighbors’ labels. Collecting the labels into \(\mathbf r\) and the quotients into \(\mathbf d\) gives the following equation.

Definition 1 (Arithmetical structure)

An arithmetical structure on \(G\) is a pair of positive integer vectors \(\mathbf d,\mathbf r\) satisfying

\[ \begin{aligned} (\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d)-A)\mathbf r&=0,\\ \gcd(r_1,\ldots,r_n)&=1. \end{aligned} \]

Primitivity of \(\mathbf r\) removes scaling. The choice \(\mathbf r=\mathbf 1\) gives the ordinary Laplacian.

Theorem 1 (Finiteness, Lorenzini)

Every finite connected loopless graph with at least two vertices has finitely many arithmetical structures [3, Lemma 1.6].

The definition gives no bound on the labels to search over. Finiteness establishes that only finitely many labelings satisfy the equations, but an explicit count requires more information about the graph. We write \(|\operatorname{Arith}(G)|\) for the number of structures.

Paths and cycles admit explicit counts, giving a starting point for the multi-edge problem. Write \(P_n\) and \(C_n\) for the path and cycle on \(n\) vertices. Both counts below distinguish labelings related by graph symmetries.

Lemma 1 (Path endpoints)

In every arithmetical structure on \(P_n\), with \(n\geq2\), the endpoint labels satisfy \(r_1=r_n=1\) [1, Lemma 1].

Theorem 2 (Paths and cycles, Braun et al.)

For paths with \(n\geq2\) and cycles with \(n\geq3\), the following counts hold [1].

\[ \begin{aligned} |\operatorname{Arith}(P_n)|&=\frac1n\binom{2n-2}{n-1},\\ |\operatorname{Arith}(C_n)|&=\binom{2n-1}{n-1}. \end{aligned} \]

The count alone does not describe the matrices obtained from these labelings. Their integral cokernels can differ even on the same graph. We study this through the critical group, the torsion subgroup of the cokernel of \(L=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d)-A\).

\[ K(G,\mathbf d,\mathbf r) =\operatorname{tors}(\mathbb Z^n/L\mathbb Z^n). \]

Theorem 3 (Critical groups of paths and cycles)

Every path structure has trivial critical group. For a cycle structure, let \(t=|\{i\mid r_i=1\}|\). Then \(t\geq1\) and [1]

\[ K(C_n,\mathbf d,\mathbf r)\cong\mathbb Z/t\mathbb Z. \]

Constructing every labeling independently would ignore the relations between different graph sizes. Subdivision gives a way to pass from one size to the next. On a cycle, inserting the sum of adjacent positive labels introduces no new unit label, so the preceding theorem also explains why the critical group is preserved.

Proposition 1 (Subdivision)

Subdivide an ordinary edge with endpoint labels \(a,b\). Give the new vertex label \(a+b\) and quotient \(1\), and increase both endpoint quotients by \(1\). This produces a normalized arithmetical structure with the same critical group [1].

Subdivision produces larger structures from known ones, but does not supply the initial structures at the multi-edge. If its endpoint labels are \(a,b\) and its multiplicity is \(k\), these three integers must be pairwise coprime [Proposition 2.1]. For such triples with \(a,b>1\), a modified Euclidean algorithm constructs two remainder chains that meet at \(1\). Choosing remainders enforces the divisibility equations as the chains are formed.

This construction chooses the cycle size through the lengths of its remainder chains. To count structures on a cycle of a given size, we must determine which endpoint data produce the required chain lengths. Our manuscript with Nesty Dogbatse and Peter Palma combines this construction with subdivision, following methods for paths with a doubled edge [2].

Open questions

A complete count at fixed cycle size and edge multiplicity requires classifying the endpoint pairs and subdivisions that produce that size, with repeated constructions accounted for. The enumeration argument in the working manuscript remains incomplete.
