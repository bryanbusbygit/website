Arithmetical Structures on Graphs

An arithmetical structure is a primitive positive-integer vertex labeling that satisfies a divisibility condition determined by adjacency. We study these structures on cycles with one multi-edge, with the aim of counting them. Smoothing and a modified Euclidean algorithm provide ways to reduce and construct examples.

Definitions and enumeration

Let \(G=(V,E)\) be a finite, connected, undirected, loopless multigraph with at least two vertices and edge multiset \(E\). For adjacent vertices \(u\) and \(v\), let \(m(u,v)\) be the number of edges joining them. An arithmetical structure assigns a positive integer \(r(v)\) to every vertex so that \(r(v)\) divides the sum of the neighboring labels, with parallel edges counted separately. Denote the positive-integer quotient by \(d(v)\).

Multiplying all labels by the same positive integer preserves the divisibility conditions. We therefore impose the normalization \(\gcd\{r(v):v\in V\}=1\). Write \(\mathbf r=(r(v))_{v\in V}\) for the vector of labels and \(\mathbf d=(d(v))_{v\in V}\) for the quotient vector. The pair \((\mathbf d,\mathbf r)\) is an arithmetical structure when

\[ (\operatorname{diag}\mathbf d-A_G)\mathbf r=0. \]

Here \(\operatorname{diag}\mathbf d\) is the diagonal matrix whose entry at vertex \(v\) is \(d(v)\), and \(A_G=(m(v,u))_{v,u\in V}\) is the adjacency matrix of \(G\). The corresponding equation at a vertex \(v\) is

\[ d(v)r(v)=\sum_{u\in V}m(v,u)r(u). \]

Here \(m(v,u)=0\) when \(u\) and \(v\) are not adjacent. Thus \(\mathbf r\) determines \(\mathbf d\), and enumeration amounts to finding the primitive positive vectors \(\mathbf r\) for which every quotient \(d(v)\) is an integer. This is the convention in Definition 1.1 of the draft manuscript.

Braun et al. enumerated arithmetical structures on paths and cycles. For a simple cycle on \(n\geq3\) labeled vertices, their Theorem 30 gives the number of structures as [1]

\[ \binom{2n-1}{n-1}. \]

Let \(\widetilde C_{n,k}\) denote the multigraph obtained by replacing one edge of an \(n\)-cycle by \(k\) parallel edges, where \(n\geq3\) and \(k\geq2\) are integers. Thus \(k\) is the total multiplicity of that edge. At either endpoint, the label at the opposite endpoint appears \(k\) times in the adjacency sum. The ordinary cycle formula does not account for this dependence on \(k\). Glass and Wagner studied the analogous enumeration problem for paths with a doubled edge. [2]

Reduction by smoothing

Suppose \(n\geq4\) and a degree-\(2\) vertex \(v\), not an endpoint of the multi-edge, has distinct neighbors \(u\) and \(w\) and satisfies \(d(v)=1\). The vertex equation gives \(r(v)=r(u)+r(w)\). Smoothing deletes \(v\), adds the edge \(uw\), and decreases \(d(u)\) and \(d(w)\) by \(1\). All remaining \(r\)-labels and other \(d\)-labels stay the same. The result is an arithmetical structure on \(\widetilde C_{n-1,k}\).

Conversely, subdivide an edge of multiplicity \(1\), with endpoints \(u,w\), by inserting a vertex \(v\). Set \(r(v)=r(u)+r(w)\) and \(d(v)=1\), and increase both endpoint \(d\)-labels by \(1\). This gives a structure on \(\widetilde C_{n+1,k}\). Both operations preserve primitivity: a common divisor of the retained labels also divides their sum. See Section 2.1 of the draft.

The draft calls a structure smooth when \(d(v)>1\) at every vertex away from the multi-edge, and strongly smooth when this holds at every vertex. Ordinary smoothing only acts away from the multi-edge, so it does not by itself ensure strong smoothness. These definitions appear in Section 3.

Constructing endpoint labels

For any arithmetical structure on \(\widetilde C_{n,k}\), the endpoint labels \(r_1,r_2\) and the multiplicity \(k\) are pairwise coprime. A common divisor of \(r_1,r_2\), or of either endpoint label and \(k\), propagates along the remaining path through the vertex equations and must divide every \(r\)-label. Primitivity forces that divisor to be \(1\). See Proposition 2.1.

Conversely, fix integers \(a,b>1\) and \(k\geq2\) that are pairwise coprime. The construction in Section 4.1 of the draft gives a structure with endpoint labels \(a,b\) on \(\widetilde C_{n,k}\) for some \(n\geq3\). The number of vertices is determined by the construction; it is not prescribed in advance.

Starting with \((kb,a)\) and \((ka,b)\), repeatedly take the least nonnegative residue of the negative of the preceding term modulo the current term, stopping before the first zero. Both sequences end in \(1\), their initial gcd. Omit the initial terms \(kb,ka\), use the remaining sequences as two paths starting at \(a,b\), join these starting vertices by \(k\) parallel edges, and identify the terminal vertices labeled \(1\). The remainder rule ensures the required divisibility at every vertex.

For \(a=7,b=5,k=4\), the sequences are \((20,7,1)\) and \((28,5,2,1)\). They give the second structure below, with \(\mathbf r=(7,5,2,1)\) in cyclic order.

The rightmost example illustrates the divisibility condition. The vertex labeled \(7\) has four edges to the vertex labeled \(5\) and one edge to the vertex labeled \(1\). Its neighboring labels contribute \(4\cdot5+1=21\), so its \(d\)-label is \(3\). The remaining vertex equations similarly determine the displayed \(\mathbf d\)-labeling.

\[ \begin{aligned} 6\cdot5&=4\cdot7+2,\\ 3\cdot2&=5+1,\\ 9\cdot1&=2+7. \end{aligned} \]
