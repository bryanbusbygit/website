Arithmetical Structures on Graphs

Arithmetical structures turn local divisibility conditions into a global enumeration problem. The count is known for ordinary cycles, but replacing one edge by parallel edges introduces new arithmetic constraints. Smoothing and a modified Euclidean algorithm reveal how the resulting structures can be reduced and reconstructed.

Where the classical count breaks

Let \(G=(V,E)\) be a finite, connected, undirected, loopless multigraph with at least two vertices and edge multiset \(E\). For adjacent vertices \(u\) and \(v\), let \(m(u,v)\) be the number of edges joining them. An arithmetical structure assigns a positive integer \(r(v)\) to every vertex so that \(r(v)\) divides the sum of the neighboring labels, with parallel edges counted separately. Denote the positive-integer quotient by \(d(v)\).

Multiplying all labels by the same positive integer preserves the divisibility conditions. The normalization \(\gcd\{r(v):v\in V\}=1\) is therefore imposed. Write \(\mathbf r=(r(v))_{v\in V}\) for the vector of labels and \(\mathbf d=(d(v))_{v\in V}\) for the quotient vector. The pair \((\mathbf d,\mathbf r)\) is an arithmetical structure when

\[ (\operatorname{diag}\mathbf d-A_G)\mathbf r=0. \]

Here \(\operatorname{diag}\mathbf d\) is the diagonal matrix whose entry at vertex \(v\) is \(d(v)\), and \(A_G=(m(v,u))_{v,u\in V}\) is the adjacency matrix of \(G\). The corresponding equation at a vertex \(v\) is

\[ d(v)r(v)=\sum_{u\in V}m(v,u)r(u). \]

Set \(m(v,u)=0\) when \(u\) and \(v\) are not adjacent. Thus \(\mathbf r\) determines \(\mathbf d\), and enumeration amounts to finding the primitive positive vectors \(\mathbf r\) for which every quotient \(d(v)\) is an integer. This is the convention in Definition 1.1 of the manuscript.

Braun et al. [1, Theorem 30] proved that, for a simple cycle on \(n\geq3\) labeled vertices, the number of structures is

\[ \binom{2n-1}{n-1}. \]

Let \(\widetilde C_{n,k}\) denote the multigraph obtained by replacing one edge of an \(n\)-cycle by \(k\) parallel edges, where \(n\geq3\) and \(k\geq2\) are integers. Thus \(k\) is the total multiplicity of that edge. At either endpoint, the label at the opposite endpoint appears \(k\) times in the adjacency sum. The ordinary cycle formula does not account for this dependence on \(k\). Glass and Wagner studied the analogous enumeration problem for paths with a doubled edge [2].

Reduction by smoothing

The multi-edge introduces arithmetic data that the length of the cycle alone cannot capture. The first step toward recovering an enumeration is to remove ordinary vertices without disturbing the essential divisibility constraints. Accordingly, suppose \(n\geq4\), and let \(v\) be a degree-\(2\) vertex that is not an endpoint of the multi-edge. If \(v\) has distinct neighbors \(u\) and \(w\) and satisfies \(d(v)=1\), then the vertex equation gives \(r(v)=r(u)+r(w)\). Smoothing deletes \(v\), adds the edge \(uw\), and decreases \(d(u)\) and \(d(w)\) by \(1\). All remaining \(r\)-labels and other \(d\)-labels stay the same. The result is an arithmetical structure on \(\widetilde C_{n-1,k}\).

Conversely, an edge of multiplicity \(1\), with endpoints \(u,w\), may be subdivided by inserting a vertex \(v\). Setting \(r(v)=r(u)+r(w)\) and \(d(v)=1\), and increasing both endpoint \(d\)-labels by \(1\), produces a structure on \(\widetilde C_{n+1,k}\). Both operations preserve primitivity: a common divisor of the retained labels also divides their sum. See Section 2.1 of the manuscript.

The manuscript defines a structure to be smooth when \(d(v)>1\) at every vertex away from the multi-edge, and strongly smooth when this holds at every vertex. Ordinary smoothing only acts away from the multi-edge, so it does not by itself ensure strong smoothness. These definitions appear in Section 3. Repeated smoothing isolates a smaller core while recording how the original cycle can be recovered by subdivision. What remains is to determine which labels may occur at the endpoints of the multi-edge and how those labels generate a valid core.

From endpoint data to a structure

For any arithmetical structure on \(\widetilde C_{n,k}\), the endpoint labels \(r_1,r_2\) and the multiplicity \(k\) are pairwise coprime. Any common divisor of \(r_1\) and \(r_2\), or of \(k\) and either endpoint label, propagates along the remaining path through the vertex equations and must divide every \(r\)-label. Primitivity forces that divisor to be \(1\). See Proposition 2.1.

Conversely, fix integers \(a,b>1\) and \(k\geq2\) that are pairwise coprime. The construction in Section 4.1 of the manuscript gives a structure with endpoint labels \(a,b\) on \(\widetilde C_{n,k}\) for some \(n\geq3\). The number of vertices is determined by the construction; it is not prescribed in advance.

Beginning with the pairs \((kb,a)\) and \((ka,b)\), form each subsequent term as the least nonnegative residue of the negative of the preceding term modulo the current term, and stop before the first zero. Pairwise coprimality implies that each sequence ends in \(1\). After omitting the initial terms \(kb\) and \(ka\), regard the remaining sequences as two paths beginning at \(a\) and \(b\). Join these initial vertices by \(k\) parallel edges and identify the terminal vertices labeled \(1\). The recurrence yields the required divisibility relation at every vertex. For \(a=7,b=5,k=4\), the sequences are \((20,7,1)\) and \((28,5,2,1)\). They give the second structure below, with \(\mathbf r=(7,5,2,1)\) in cyclic order.

In the rightmost example, the vertex labeled \(7\) has four edges to the vertex labeled \(5\) and one edge to the vertex labeled \(1\). Its neighboring labels contribute \(4\cdot5+1=21\), so its \(d\)-label is \(3\). The remaining vertex equations similarly determine the displayed \(\mathbf d\)-labeling.

\[ \begin{aligned} 6\cdot5&=4\cdot7+2,\\ 3\cdot2&=5+1,\\ 9\cdot1&=2+7. \end{aligned} \]

The construction converts a global labeling problem into two finite remainder sequences. Coprimality determines whether the sequences reach \(1\), and smoothing determines how the resulting core expands into larger cycles. Together, these operations separate the arithmetic imposed by the multi-edge from the combinatorics of subdivision. For additional background, see this video introduction to arithmetical structures.
