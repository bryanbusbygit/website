Arithmetical Structures on Graphs

Arithmetical structures generalize the graph Laplacian by replacing vertex degrees with positive integers while retaining a positive integer kernel vector. Originating in Lorenzini's study of degenerating curves, they connect integer linear algebra with graph enumeration [3]. This project studies cycles with one multi-edge through subdivision and a modified Euclidean algorithm.

Let \(G\) be a finite connected undirected graph on vertices \(v_1,\ldots,v_n\), with \(n\geq2\). We allow parallel edges between two vertices but no loops from a vertex to itself. Its adjacency matrix \(A\) has entry \(A_{ij}\) equal to the number of edges between \(v_i\) and \(v_j\). The degree \(\deg(v_i)\) counts incident edges, including their multiplicities. The ordinary graph Laplacian is \(\operatorname{diag}(\deg(v_1),\ldots,\deg(v_n))-A\), where \(\operatorname{diag}\) places its arguments on the diagonal and has zeros elsewhere.

The path \(P_n\) joins \(n\) vertices in a chain; the cycle \(C_n\), for \(n\geq3\), also joins the last vertex to the first. In \(\widetilde C_{n,k}\), one edge of \(C_n\) is replaced by \(k\geq2\) parallel edges, called the multi-edge. The remaining edges have multiplicity \(1\) and are called ordinary edges.

Definition 1 (Arithmetical structure)

An arithmetical structure on \(G\) is a pair of positive integer vectors \(\mathbf d=(d_1,\ldots,d_n)\) and \(\mathbf r=(r_1,\ldots,r_n)\) satisfying

\[ \begin{aligned} (\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d)-A)\mathbf r&=0,\\ \gcd(r_1,\ldots,r_n)&=1. \end{aligned} \]

The first condition makes \(\mathbf r\) a kernel vector, meaning that multiplying it by the matrix gives zero. The second says its entries have greatest common divisor \(1\); such a vector is called primitive or normalized.

Each \(r\)-label divides the sum of its neighbors, counting edge multiplicity, and the quotient is its \(d\)-label. Thus the equation at \(v_i\) is \(d_i r_i=\sum_{j=1}^n A_{ij}r_j\). Normalization removes scaled copies of \(\mathbf r\). The assignment \(r_i=1\), \(d_i=\deg(v_i)\) recovers the ordinary Laplacian. Write \(\operatorname{Arith}(G)\) for the set of normalized structures on \(G\), and \(|\operatorname{Arith}(G)|\) for its size.

Theorem 1 (Finiteness, Lorenzini)

Every finite connected loopless graph with at least two vertices has finitely many arithmetical structures [3, Lemma 1.6].

The labels are positive integers with no bound imposed in the definition, so finiteness is already a substantive restriction. It turns the existence problem into an enumeration problem. How does the shape of a graph determine the number of allowable labelings?

Paths and ordinary cycles provide the basic comparison. Here \(P_n\) and \(C_n\) have fixed vertex positions; labelings related by a reflection or rotation are not identified.

Lemma 1 (Path endpoints)

In every arithmetical structure on \(P_n\), with \(n\geq2\), the endpoint labels satisfy \(r_1=r_n=1\) [1, Lemma 1].

Theorem 2 (Paths and cycles, Braun et al.)

For \(n\geq2\), path structures are counted by a Catalan number; for \(n\geq3\), cycle structures satisfy [1].

\[ \begin{aligned} |\operatorname{Arith}(P_n)|&=\frac1n\binom{2n-2}{n-1},\\ |\operatorname{Arith}(C_n)|&=\binom{2n-1}{n-1}. \end{aligned} \]

For example, the two structures on \(P_3\) are \(\mathbf r=(1,1,1)\) with \(\mathbf d=(1,2,1)\), and \(\mathbf r=(1,2,1)\) with \(\mathbf d=(2,1,2)\). Closing the path into a triangle gives \(|\operatorname{Arith}(C_3)|=10\). Even one added edge changes the possible integer labelings substantially.

Counting structures is one question; understanding their algebra is another. Put \(L=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d)-A\). The cokernel \(\mathbb Z^n/L\mathbb Z^n\) groups integer vectors together when their difference is \(L\mathbf z\) for some integer vector \(\mathbf z\). Its torsion subgroup consists of the classes that become zero after multiplication by some positive integer. This defines the critical group

\[ K(G,\mathbf d,\mathbf r) =\operatorname{tors}(\mathbb Z^n/L\mathbb Z^n). \]

A trivial group has only its zero element. The cyclic group \(\mathbb Z/t\mathbb Z\) is the integers under addition modulo \(t\); the symbol \(\cong\) means that two groups have the same group structure.

Theorem 3 (Critical groups of paths and cycles)

Every path structure has trivial critical group. For a cycle structure, let \(t=|\{i\mid r_i=1\}|\). Then \(t\geq1\) and [1]

\[ K(C_n,\mathbf d,\mathbf r)\cong\mathbb Z/t\mathbb Z. \]

On \(C_3\), the Laplacian structure \(\mathbf r=(1,1,1)\) has critical group \(\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z\). The structure \(\mathbf r=(1,1,2)\), \(\mathbf d=(3,3,1)\) has group \(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\), while \(\mathbf r=(1,2,3)\), \(\mathbf d=(5,2,1)\) has trivial group. Thus the same graph supports different critical groups as its arithmetic data vary.

A multi-edge changes the divisibility conditions at its two endpoints. To isolate this arithmetic, the remaining path can be studied through subdivision, which replaces an edge by two edges meeting at a new vertex. Its inverse, smoothing, removes a vertex of degree two and joins its neighbors.

Proposition 1 (Subdivision)

Subdivide an ordinary edge with endpoint labels \(a,b\). Give the new vertex label \(a+b\) and quotient \(1\), and increase both endpoint quotients by \(1\). This produces a normalized arithmetical structure with the same critical group [1].

At an endpoint, the neighbor sum increases by its own label, explaining the increment in its quotient. At the inserted vertex, the neighbor sum equals its label, so its quotient is \(1\).

On a multi-edge cycle, an ordinary vertex is one not incident to the multi-edge. Smoothing removes such a vertex with \(d_i=1\), decreasing both neighbor quotients by \(1\). It applies away from the multi-edge when at least four vertices remain before removal. Structures whose ordinary vertices all have quotients greater than \(1\) are smooth. If every quotient, including those at the multi-edge endpoints, exceeds \(1\), the structure is strongly smooth.

The endpoint data constrain which reduced structures can occur. If \(a,b\) label the multi-edge endpoints and \(k\) is its multiplicity, then \(a,b,k\) must be pairwise coprime, meaning that each pair has gcd \(1\) [Proposition 2.1]. Conversely, pairwise coprime \(a,b>1\), \(k\geq2\) determine a construction. Starting from \((kb,a)\) and \((ka,b)\), append to consecutive terms \(p,q\) the least nonnegative remainder \(s\equiv-p\pmod q\), stopping before zero. The equation \(p+s=dq\) enforces local divisibility. Discarding the initial terms, joining \(a,b\) by \(k\) edges, and identifying the terminal \(1\)'s gives a cycle with the prescribed endpoints.

This construction supplies labelings, but its remainder sequences determine the cycle's size. Enumeration at a fixed size therefore requires classifying endpoint data and subdivisions that produce a prescribed size without duplication. The manuscript with Nesty Dogbatse and Peter Palma develops this approach, extending methods used for paths with a doubled edge [2].

What we don’t know

For the multi-edge project, the next step is to turn the endpoint construction into a complete count for prescribed \(n\) and \(k\). Which endpoint pairs yield a smooth structure of the required size, and how can their subdivisions be counted without duplication? The construction above gives examples, but does not by itself provide a counting formula. The enumeration section of the working manuscript develops this reduction; its general argument remains to be completed.

A related question concerns the starting structures for that reduction. Can strongly smooth structures be generated from a classified collection of smaller ones, with precise conditions for reversing each operation? The triple-edge case in the manuscript leaves this origin question unresolved. Settling it would explain both which initial structures must be counted and whether different construction histories lead to the same labeling.
